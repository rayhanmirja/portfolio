# Auditing My Life for Friction

Right now I'm running a YouTube channel, a second channel for coding, a software engineering degree, an agency, and a language I started from zero. Each one is manageable alone. Together they leave almost no slack, and when there's no slack, small problems stop being small.

A shoot can start late because one battery wasn't charged. Flashcard reviews pile up because I skipped a single night. A plan stays in my head because writing it down felt like one more task.

My first instinct is always to push harder. That's usually the wrong tool. So I'm doing something more systematic: a **lifestyle audit**, in public.

---

## Where the Idea Comes From

I'm borrowing this exercise from Nick Saraev, who ran one on his own life, published the results, and teaches the method inside Maker School. He credits a LessWrong article on "bughunting" as his starting point. I've rewritten the steps in my own words and run them on my own life.

---

## Friction, Defined

Friction is whatever stands between me and a thing I've already decided to do.

* If my tripod, mic, and light live in three different places, every shoot begins with a scavenger hunt. That's friction, and it quietly makes me shoot less.
* If I only ever study vocabulary at my desk, then every free ten minutes elsewhere is wasted. That's friction too.
* If dinner means deciding from scratch every night, I'll eat whatever is closest, not whatever fits my targets.

None of these are character flaws. My environment is voting against me, and I keep letting it win. Behavioral economists call the broader idea **choice architecture**: the layout of your surroundings shapes which options feel natural. Companies design offices and menus around it. Almost nobody designs their own day around it.

The fix isn't more discipline. It's rearranging things so the behavior I want is the one that takes the least effort.

---

## How the Audit Works

1. **List the friction.** Walk through every area of life and write down every point, unfiltered.
2. **Attach a possible fix** to each one.
3. **Score the difficulty** from 1 to 10. A 1 means I could fix it in the next five minutes. A 10 means it needs a real decision or serious money.
4. **Work from easiest to hardest.** Quick wins build momentum, and clearing small items makes the big ones less crowded.

Steps 1 to 3 take about an hour. Step 4 stretches over weeks, and it works better spread out: a few items a day, not everything in one weekend.

Two rules matter. First, while listing, don't try to solve anything. Second, writing a problem down is not a promise to fix it. Plenty of people avoid listing problems because they assume naming one means they owe it action. It doesn't.

---

## The Six Prompts

Set a five-minute timer for each one and write until it rings.

* **Walkthrough.** Replay a normal day in your head, from waking up to sleeping. Where do you stall, rush, or decide the same thing again?
* **Habits and skills.** What do you want to do more, less, or differently? Which skills are stuck, and why?
* **Space.** Look at your desk, your room, your bag, your devices. What annoys you every day that you've stopped noticing?
* **Time and attention.** What drains your energy, eats your hours, or keeps pulling you away from the work?
* **Blind spots.** Imagine a blunt friend reviewing your life. What would they say you're avoiding or overestimating?
* **Fear.** What are you putting off because thinking about it is uncomfortable?

## My Friction List

Each item below is marked **Solved**, **In progress**, or **Open**. I'll keep updating this post as things move.

### Walkthrough

**1. My shoots had no fixed day.** Filming "when I feel ready" meant filming less.
**Solved.** Shooting now happens Monday morning (starting at 7:30) and Friday at 4 PM. Videos go out Monday and Thursday at 7 PM. The calendar decides, not my mood.

**2. Dinner is a decision every night.** Hitting a daily protein target with local food is easy when I know what to eat and tiring when I don't.
**In progress.** Building a short rotation of Bangladeshi meals that already fit my numbers, so deciding becomes picking from a list.

**3. No protected block for deep work.** A degree, two channels, an agency, and Japanese all compete for the same hours.
**Open.** Plan: each week, name one priority and give it two protected blocks before anything else is scheduled.

### Habits and Skills

**4. Japanese reviews pile up when I skip a day.**
**In progress.** I'm capping new cards at about ten a day and attaching the session to something I already do every day, so it stops depending on motivation.

**5. Six training days a week leaves no slack.** One missed session either disappears or guilts me into doubling up.
**Open.** Plan: treat the split as a queue (push, pull, legs) rather than fixed weekdays, so a missed day shifts the schedule instead of breaking it.

**6. Two channels, one person.** The coding channel competes with Carfinity for the same editing hours.
**In progress.** Consolidating the coding content into a single channel, with a lighter release schedule than Carfinity.

### Space

**7. Setup time before every shoot.** Phone, Canon, mic, light panel: gathering them costs ten minutes and some motivation each time.
**Open.** Plan: one packed shoot bag, a short checklist, and everything charged the night before.

**8. Starting every edit from scratch.**
**Solved.** I built a reusable DaVinci Resolve project with my audio chain and standard settings, so each edit starts at the cut instead of at the setup.

### Time and Attention

**9. Marketplace freelancing drained hours and returned nothing.**
**Solved.** I stopped Upwork and Fiverr and narrowed my focus to two things: YouTube and the agency.

**10. A blank page every week for LinkedIn.**
**In progress.** A fixed post structure, plus an ideas list that lives next to the camera so I capture notes while I'm already working.

### Blind Spots

**11. I track output instead of outcomes.** Shipping on schedule feels like progress, but it only matters if it moves the actual goal: 5,000 subscribers in six months.
**Open.** Plan: a weekly check on that one number, written down before I plan the next week's content.

### Fear

**12. The long-term plan lives in my head.** Finishing the degree, moving abroad for part of it, and a master's afterward all carry real costs and deadlines, and I haven't put them in one place.
**Open.** Plan: a single spreadsheet with costs, dates, and deadlines, reviewed monthly.

## What Happens Next

That's the list as it stands today. I'll update each item's status as it moves and add a short note on what worked and what didn't. Several of these will probably change shape once I try them, and that's the point of writing them down.

If you run your own audit, I'd like to hear what surfaced. Start with the smallest item on your list.
